Short, plain lessons on staying safe on the phone, in your email, and on the internet. No jargon, no tests you can fail. Start by trying the practice below.

Five short lessons

Tap a lesson to open it. Take them in any order.

1. Phone calls that feel urgent
  • Hang up if you feel rushed. The bank, Medicare, the IRS and Social Security will never threaten arrest or demand money on the spot.
  • Caller ID can be faked. A call that shows your bank's name may not be your bank.
  • A "grandchild" in trouble? Hang up and call your family member on the number you already have. Agree on a secret family code word ahead of time.
  • Only scammers ask for gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency or cash pickup. No real business or agency does.
  • It is polite to hang up. You do not owe a stranger an explanation.
2. Emails and text messages
  • Don't tap links in messages you weren't expecting. Instead, type the website address yourself or open the company's app.
  • Clues of a scam: urgency ("act within 24 hours"), spelling mistakes, an odd sender address, or a request for a password, Social Security number or card number.
  • Don't reply to unknown texts, not even "STOP". Replying tells them your number is real. Delete the text, or forward it to 7726 (SPAM) to report it.
  • Unexpected attachments can hide harmful software. If you didn't expect it, don't open it.
3. Passwords and sign-in codes
  • Use a few random words. Something like pine-teapot-river-lamp is stronger and easier to remember than a short jumble of symbols.
  • Never reuse your email or bank password. If one is stolen, the others stay safe.
  • A written notebook kept at home is fine. A thief online can't read it. A password manager app is also a good choice.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for email and banking. You get a code on your phone when you sign in.
  • Never read that code to anyone who contacts you. Anyone asking for it is a scammer.
4. Shopping and banking online
  • Shop at stores you already know. If a deal looks too good to be true, it usually is.
  • Use a credit card, not a debit card. Credit cards usually offer stronger protection if something goes wrong.
  • "Your computer has a virus" pop-up? Close the window or restart. Microsoft and Apple never put a phone number in a warning. Don't call it.
  • Check your statements each month for charges you don't recognize.
  • Avoid banking on free public Wi-Fi at cafés and hotels.
5. Updates and your devices
  • Install updates. They fix security holes. Turn on automatic updates in your phone or computer settings.
  • Use a screen lock (a PIN or fingerprint) on your phone and tablet.
  • Never let a stranger take control of your computer, even if they say they are "from tech support".
  • Ask someone you trust before installing a new app or program.

Three rules to remember

1. Slow down.Urgency is the scammer's main tool. A real problem can wait ten minutes.
2. Check another way.Hang up, then call the number on your card, your statement, or the company's own website.
3. Never pay with gift cards, wire transfers or crypto.Those are the payments scammers ask for, because they can't be taken back.

If something already happened

It is not your fault. Smart, careful people get caught every day, and acting quickly helps.

  1. Stop talking to the person. Don't send more money.
  2. Call your bank or card company using the number on the back of your card.
  3. Change passwords for any account you shared, starting with email.
  4. Tell someone you trust, such as a family member or friend.
  5. Report it. Reports help stop the scammers.
